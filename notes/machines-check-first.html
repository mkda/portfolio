Machines check first: what running an AI studio taught me about review gates

This year I built a small studio that makes children's cartoons with AI agents. Agents write, draw, animate, voice, check and publish each episode. I approve the story, any new characters and the final cut. It's a product like any other: there's a pipeline, a budget and an audience that decides. The most useful lesson has nothing to do with models. It's about where quality actually comes from.

More review made things worse

When an episode disappointed me, my first reaction was the one many teams have: add more review. More passes, more feedback rounds, more versions. On one episode that loop produced 84 versions and about $31 of extra generation, and the result was no better. I rolled the process back.

The problem wasn't too little review. Review happened too late, by the most expensive reviewer (me), on problems a machine could have caught.

Let machines catch the boring problems

So I turned the order around. Before any person looks at a cut, automated checks run for staging, continuity and lip-sync, followed by three independent reviewer agents for assets, continuity and motion. On one episode the automated staging checks went from 173 issues to zero before I saw a single frame.

That left the human gates for what only a person can judge. Is the story right for a three-year-old? Does a new character belong in this world? Is this cut good enough to publish? There are two gates, one before any paid generation and one before release, and nothing else needs me.

Every note becomes a rule

When I did give feedback, I logged it. It fell into nine categories that kept coming back. Each one became a rule or an automated check, and a repeat of any of them is now a blocker. Feedback that becomes a check gets cheaper every episode. Feedback that stays a comment costs the same every time.

Pay per usable result

The same thinking changed how I buy models. Price per call is misleading. What matters is price divided by pass rate. Measured that way, the same image model through a different provider cost $0.12 per usable drawing instead of $0.18, a third less for identical quality. Video models went through the same test on the studio's own characters: two others passed none of six test clips between them, and the one now on trial passed three of three for less money.

What this means outside a studio

  • Put automated checks before human review, not after.
  • Keep human gates few, early and about judgment, not inspection.
  • Turn repeated feedback into rules, and rules into checks.
  • Compare vendors on the cost of a usable result, not the list price.

None of this is specific to AI. It's how I've run products with physical goods, payments and e-commerce too. The agents just make the feedback loop fast enough to see it in weeks instead of years.
