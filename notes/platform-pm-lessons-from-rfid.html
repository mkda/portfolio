Platform PM lessons from RFID

Mikael Dell'Anna · 15 April 2026 · 6 min read

For the past two years I've led the RFID technology workstream at a global sportswear brand: tagged products moving from the factory, through distribution, to more than 100 stores, feeding a Digital Twin platform that links product, ERP and retail systems. It's hardware, data and software at once, and a platform in the full sense: many teams depend on it, and none of them sees all of it. These are the lessons I'd pass on.

1. A platform has many customers, and they compete

Supply chain wanted shipment and receiving visibility. Retail wanted counts, search and replenishment. Logistics partners needed scan points that just worked. Every request was reasonable, and capacity was fixed. I used WSJF, and RICE where it fitted better, to compare supply-chain tracking features with retail features against the engineering we had budgeted and our progress on the roadmap. The scores didn't make the decision, but they made the trade-off visible, so saying no became a shared conclusion rather than a personal one.

2. Standards are product decisions

Modelling the Digital Twin on Electronic Product Code events, and integrating Global Location Numbers across the supply chain, looked like technical details. In practice they decided how fast new partners and use cases could join. An event-driven architecture turned more than 40 million scans a year into data other systems could subscribe to, instead of reports someone had to request.

3. Observe before you model

Interviews told us how stores managed inventory. Watching them showed us how stores really did it, including how they used virtual locations. That observation meant rebuilding the data model for the retail RFID integration. It was painful, but far cheaper than discovering it after rollout.

4. Exceptions are the product

A traceability rate above 99.9% sounds finished. But the remaining fraction, multiplied across 100+ locations, generated most of the work. We sorted support tickets with a Pareto analysis and combined them with stakeholder interviews to find the few features that kept engagement and usability right. Together with event-driven data and a global support framework, that cut manual interventions by 70%, with 500+ FTE days a year in projected savings.

5. Insource what you need to control

Some capabilities sat with vendors. Where the data was core to the platform, we moved capabilities in-house and decommissioned vendor setups. Self-managed serialisation was one of them: its MVP alone was projected to save more than $200K a year, and the bigger gain was owning the data model.

6. Write it down, and map the dependencies

I wrote BRDs and PRDs for seven connected initiatives: self-managed serialisation, a retail RFID dashboard, warehouse scan-point API automation, GLN supply-chain automation, the event-driven EPC architecture, vendor decommissioning, and product authentication. Story mapping sessions kept teams focused on user journeys. A shared dependency map kept seven initiatives from blocking each other.
