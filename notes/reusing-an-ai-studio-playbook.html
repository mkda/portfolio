Reusing an AI studio's playbook in enterprise automation

Mikael Dell'Anna · 18 August 2026 · 6 min read

This summer I built a small studio that makes children's cartoons with AI agents. An orchestrating agent plans the work, specialised agents draw, animate, voice, check and publish, and a worker machine runs the paid jobs. I approve the story, any new characters and the final cut. Six episodes later, in six languages, I've noticed that almost everything I learned applies well beyond cartoons. It's the same playbook I'd bring to any company that wants to automate with AI.

1. Machines check first

Before any person reviews a cut, automated checks run for staging, continuity, lip-sync and joins between shots, followed by three independent reviewer agents. On one episode, the staging checks took the count from 173 issues to zero before I watched a single frame.

In your organisation, run rule and model checks on every order, ticket, scan or document, and send people the exceptions. In retail RFID (radio-frequency identification) this is the difference between store teams checking every count and store teams handling exceptions.

2. Gates only where judgment matters

When an episode disappointed me, I reacted like many teams do and added review. One episode went to 84 versions and about $31 of extra generation for the same quality, so I went back to two gates: one before any money is spent, and one before release.

A company can do the same: put approvals on decisions such as spend, customer impact and compliance, and let routine steps flow. In a telecom checkout that means automating ID checks and activation, and routing only the doubtful cases to a person.

3. Pay per usable result

AI services bill you for wrong outputs too. I compare models on price divided by pass rate. The same image model through a different provider cost $0.12 per usable drawing instead of $0.18, a third less for identical quality.

Measure cost per successful task for each vendor, such as a ticket resolved in one go or a document extracted correctly first time. That number tells you far more than list price.

4. Test on your own data

Every model is tried on the studio's own characters before it's adopted. The video model now on trial passed three of three test clips at a lower cost per clip, while two others passed none of six. One defect I expected an AI edit to solve was handled better by a small script: the AI kept 4 of 15 drawings clean, the script fixed all 15 for free.

Build an eval set from your real tickets, products or contracts before you sign with a vendor, and keep plain code in the comparison. Your own data is the evidence that counts.

5. Every note becomes a rule

I logged my feedback. It fell into nine categories that kept coming back, and each one became a rule or an automated check. Repeating one is now a blocker.

In your teams, turn recurring support and QA (quality assurance) findings into checks, so quality compounds with every release instead of resetting.

6. Guard the budget and the licences

Every paid call is logged against a $100 cap per episode, and anything over about $10 comes to me first. Licences are checked too: one voice model sounded clearer than my choice, but its weights were licensed for non-commercial use only, so I kept my choice for production.

Before scaling, set spending caps and escalation thresholds per workflow, and make legal and licence checks part of model selection from day one.

7. The weakest part sets the limit

The whole pipeline runs only as fast as its weakest component. My studio once depended on a single Mac mini, and prepaid credit running dry paused jobs silently. Updates are the other risk: a new model version or tool update can quietly break a flow that worked yesterday.

For any automation, load-test every step, monitor and alert on every stop, and treat each vendor model update like a release. Revalidate the workflow and watch it in production before you call the update a success.

All of this is good product management. Agents just make the feedback loop fast enough to see these lessons in weeks instead of years.
