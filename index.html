Three problems I'm good at

Putting AI to work without losing control

Most AI pilots fail between the demo and daily use. I design the part around the model: what it may decide, what code decides, how errors get caught, and what each good result costs.

How I approach it

Models interpret; software holds the permissions. Automated checks run before any person looks. People decide at a few gates, where judgment matters. Every model is tested on our own data, and when evidence is weak the system waits for more.

Where I've done it

I built and run an animation studio where agents write, draw, animate, voice and publish children's episodes in six languages, with two human approval gates. Separately, I built an experimental decision-support platform that abstains on weak evidence and keeps orders, sizing and accounting under code control.

Connecting physical products to data

Hardware, partners and legacy systems all have to agree on what a single item is and where it is. I build the platform that makes them agree, and treat its APIs and events as products other teams can rely on.

How it works in practice

Each item gets a unique serial, encoded in its RFID tag. Every enabled read point, from factory to store, can trace it one by one or in bulk, and the Digital Twin keeps the ERP in sync for financial transactions and inventory status. A QR code paired with the same serial carries the item past the sale, into digital workflows such as its status changing from in sale to sold.

What I owned

The RFID technology workstream for a global sportswear brand: the event-driven Electronic Product Code architecture, warehouse scan-point APIs, supply-chain location standards, a self-managed serialisation service and bringing vendor capabilities in-house.

  • >99.9% item traceability across 100+ stores
  • −70% manual interventions once scans became events
  • $200K+ a year projected savings from the in-house serialisation MVP

Growth that holds up in front of finance

I like growth work that can be proved twice: once in the funnel, and once in the business case that paid for it.

In the funnel

At a Swiss telecom operator I tied every test to a KPI and a tracking plan, then ran A/B tests across subscriptions, prepaid, internet and devices, through checkout, ID checks, activation, upgrades and renewals. Session recordings and customer feedback explained the why behind the numbers.

In the business case

For engineering investments I write NPV-based requests over three to five years, with break-even and yearly profitability, and value drivers finance can check, such as loss prevention, recalls and compliance.

  • 150% year-on-year online sales growth in 2022, and growth again in 2023
  • 130 stores covered by a 3-year RFID business case

Teams, vendors and trade-offs

Teams and budgets

I've led about 20 engineers through a year-long move to microservices, run a workstream with 5 developers, 3 vendors and a CHF 250K+ budget, and managed a web team of 4 that shipped 300+ campaigns a year.

Making trade-offs visible

When supply chain and retail both needed the same platform, I scored their requests with WSJF against the engineering we actually had. The scores opened the conversation. With the trade-off in plain view, both teams reached the priority together.

Watching the real work

Interviews told us how stores handled inventory. Observation showed how they really did it, including virtual locations, and led us to rebuild the data model before rollout, when the change was cheap.

Writing it down

BRDs for the business, PRDs for the team, story mapping to keep journeys in view, and one dependency map across seven connected initiatives so all of them kept moving.

What running AI in production taught me

Each lesson came with a measured result in my studio, and each one applies to retail operations, telecom, customer service and internal processes.
