01 · How I work

Five capabilities, and where I've used them

Company names are left out on purpose. The methods and the results are real.

01

Measure what matters, then test it

I start with the outcome metric, then the leading indicators and guardrails around it, and write them into a tracking plan before anything ships. Dashboards come after the questions, not before. For AI features the same discipline becomes evals: a fixed test set from your own data, automated scorers, a pass threshold, and cost per successful result instead of cost per call. Where hindsight can fool you, I use point-in-time data, sealed holdouts and prospective observation before anything counts as ready.

  • KPI trees & tracking plans
  • Looker
  • A/B testing
  • Optimizely · Adobe Target
  • Hotjar · Clarity · Medallia
  • Eval sets & scorers
  • Cost per usable result
  • 150% year-on-year online sales growth, with A/B tests running across subscriptions, prepaid, internet, devices, checkout, ID checks, activation, upgrades and renewals
  • 173 → 0 staging issues caught by automated checks before human review, in my AI studio
  • −33% cost per usable drawing ($0.12 vs $0.18) by comparing AI providers on price divided by pass rate
02

Make the case in money, then prioritise against capacity

A roadmap item earns its place through a business case. I build NPV-based CAPEX requests over a 3 to 5-year horizon, with break-even and profitability by year, and I name the value drivers finance can check. Once funded, I prioritise with WSJF or RICE against the engineering capacity we actually have, and use Pareto analysis of support tickets and stakeholder interviews to find the few fixes that matter most.

  • NPV & discount rates
  • Break-even & payback
  • ROI models
  • CAPEX requests
  • WSJF · RICE
  • Pareto analysis
  • 130 stores covered by a 3-year business case across loss prevention, recalls and Digital Product Passport compliance
  • $200K+ a year in projected savings from a self-managed serialisation MVP
  • WSJF used to balance supply-chain tracking against retail features on a shared Digital Twin platform
03

Find the real need, then write it down so teams can build it

Interviews and observation almost always change the picture, because they surface latent needs nobody wrote down. I then use techniques like SCAMPER to meet those needs in the leanest way, and capture the result in a BRD for the business and a PRD for the team, with story mapping and dependency mapping so several initiatives can move together.

  • Interviews & observation
  • Jobs to be done
  • SCAMPER
  • BRD · PRD
  • User story mapping
  • Dependency mapping
  • Data model rebuilt after observing how stores really managed inventory and locations, before the retail RFID integration went further
  • 7 initiatives specified with BRDs and PRDs: serialisation, retail RFID dashboard, warehouse scan-point APIs, GLN supply-chain automation, event-driven EPC architecture, vendor decommissioning, product authentication
04

Build platforms other teams can rely on

A platform has many customers: supply chain, retail, logistics partners and the systems behind them. I manage APIs and events as products, protect reliability, and say no when a request would bend the platform out of shape. Bringing capabilities in-house from vendors is part of that, when it lowers cost and gives us control over the data.

  • APIs & integrations
  • Event-driven architecture
  • EPC · GLN standards
  • RFID · Digital Twin
  • Microservices
  • Vendor insourcing
  • >99.9% item traceability from factory to 100+ stores
  • 40M+ scans a year turned into event-driven omnichannel data, −70% manual interventions
  • ~12 months to move a high-volume sales portal and order flow to microservices with ~20 engineers, while it kept selling
05

Put AI to work, with guardrails

AI output is probabilistic, so the product question is which errors are acceptable and how the system catches the rest. I design agent workflows with an orchestrator and specialised agents, automated checks before any human, approval gates only where judgment is needed, spending caps, and a rule that every update is revalidated and monitored before it counts as done. I choose models by testing them on our own data, and check their licences before they reach production. Model output informs a decision but never acts alone: structured outputs, evidence with source and freshness, and abstention when the evidence isn't good enough.

02 · From my studio to your automation

What running an AI studio teaches any automation initiative

My studio is a small but complete AI operation: agents, paid models, a budget, a schedule and an audience. Each lesson below came with a measured result, and each one carries over to retail operations, telecom, customer service and internal processes.

In the studioMeasured resultIn your organisation
Machines check first. Automated staging, continuity and lip-sync checks run before anyone reviews a cut.173 issues to zero before human reviewRun rule and model checks on every order, ticket or document, so people only review what fails. Store teams handle exceptions, not every scan.
Gates only where judgment matters. Two human approvals: before money is spent, and before release.A heavier review loop ran to 84 versions with no better result, and was rolled backPut approvals on decisions (spend, customer impact, compliance), not on routine steps. Fewer gates, decided faster.
Pay per usable result. Models are compared on price divided by pass rate.A third cheaper per usable drawing, same qualityMeasure cost per successful task for each AI vendor, such as an ID check that passed or a ticket resolved without a human handover.
Test on your own data. Every model is tried on the studio's own characters before adoption.Two models passed 0 of 6 test clips; the one on trial passed 3 of 3 for lessBuild an eval set from your real tickets, products or contracts before signing with a vendor. Demos aren't evidence.
Every note becomes a rule. Repeated feedback fell into nine categories, and each became a check.Repeating a known issue is now a blockerTurn recurring support and QA findings into automated checks, so quality improves with every release instead of resetting.
Budget guards. Every paid call is logged against a per-episode cap.$100 cap per episode; spend over ~$10 needs approvalSet spending caps and escalation thresholds per workflow before scaling usage.
The weakest part sets the limit. If one component can't take the load, the whole pipeline stops, and updates can break working flows.Every model or tool update is revalidated and monitored before it's declared doneLoad-test each step, add monitoring and alerts, and treat vendor model updates like releases with regression tests.
